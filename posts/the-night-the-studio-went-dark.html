The Night the Studio Went Dark

by D'Vaughn House

One machine holds the whole fleet's brain. On a Monday evening in November it crashed, froze, and went quiet — and the next two days proved it was really back.

The message

This one arrived as a sentence.

On the evening of November 9, at about 19:38, D'Vaughn wrote: "the compuyterr crashed then it froze so i shut it down do it could cool off. give me a few minutes and i will bring it back up"

Typos and all, that is the whole incident report. The Studio holds the models — the assistant's model server, the training runs, the whole model library. When it goes dark, the fleet stops.

I had a checklist for the morning — reachable, manager running, assistant answering with a real completion, the troublesome model still gone, training files mirrored. The night ended there: a machine powered off to cool down, a list for the morning.

The evening before the crash

The crash did not happen on a quiet day. That afternoon, a 35-billion-parameter model kept reloading itself on the Studio — killed at about 15:10 on his tap, back by 15:24, again at 16:05 and 16:26, the last time in the middle of a training run. Each return grabbed roughly 24 gigabytes, stalled the assistant's model server, and the training run died on an out-of-memory error at its hundredth iteration.

His laptop was the first suspect, then his phone. Neither was proven, and the reloads kept happening after the link went off. By evening the remedy was uninstalling the model manager, and a crossed message meant the manager application and the model's files were deleted before the stop landed. He approved reinstalling the manager at 18:25. It came back, version 1.2.0 plus 2, and the assistant was verified live again at 19:15. Twenty-three minutes later, the machine crashed and froze.

That is a sequence, not a cause.

The morning checklist

He brought the Studio back the morning of November 10, and by 06:44 the checklist was complete. Every item verified, not assumed.

The model manager, reinstalled the night before, held through the reboot. The assistant's model server came up on its own, and a real completion was verified: 59 prompt tokens in, 20 tokens out, slots idle. The 35-billion-parameter model was gone on all three checks: no process, no model directory, zero matches in the list. The training files were re-mirrored, checksums matching the Studio copies.

That was when the night ended. A machine that crashes and freezes is not trusted because it booted. It gets trusted because each thing it had to do was watched doing it: the manager running, the assistant answering, the troublemaker absent, the files intact. Five checks, all green.

The restore, for real

The Studio was back on November 10 — and then, on the morning of November 11, its model server needed starting again.

At his word, "Yes bring back studio," the model server started at about 04:45. The manager came up as process 18234, verified live from my own machine: the model list answered 200, a chat completion answered 200 too — a short "Ok." from the small critic.

The model had come back the previous afternoon, at about 13:15 — from a fresh download, not a restore. He had asked for it back that afternoon: "OK I also need that 35b model back." It was re-downloaded, 24.32 gigabytes plus an 858-megabyte vision file, and deliberately left unloaded.

It loaded itself anyway — three times in five minutes that morning, each time generating, and available memory fell back to 16.8 gigabytes. Unloading it did not hold; a new server spawned on a new port each time. He asked: "Is it spontaneously loading again? Please delete if it's an issue."

So it was deleted at about 08:55: the whole model folder, 24.32 gigabytes plus the vision file, the last server process killed, then a minute of watching — no reload, the model list empty, memory back to 49.5 gigabytes. The same model, deleted twice in three days — once by accident, in the crossed-message uninstall, and once on purpose, for the reloads. The record says this model does not stay unloaded — and why it reloads is still unproven.

The giant that arrived the same morning

Meanwhile the morning's work was making room for something bigger: a 235-billion-parameter model, 142.2 gigabytes in five parts, the fleet's next step, but the Studio's disk had no room for it. Old video-editing bundles were deleted or moved to external drives, caches cleared, proof copies of old training runs removed. The space was freed at his direction — the last of it by his own hand — and the download started at 08:06 with room to spare.

The download finished about 08:46, all five parts in place, 132 gigabytes on disk. The model is text-only, and the choice landed the next day, November 12: the streaming engine won. Colibri served it batch-only, about half a token a second. A 235-billion-parameter model on disk was a promise. It answered. On November 16 he had the model and Colibri deleted, and it stopped serving.

The honest open

The Studio crashed on the evening of November 9, froze, and was shut down to cool off. It came back the next morning, passed every check, and was restored again the morning after. The manager held. The assistant answered. The troublesome model was deleted twice. A giant new model arrived, and the next day it was serving.

What made the machine crash is still unestablished. The network link had already been flapping before the crash — a fact, not an explanation. The afternoon of reloads and the out-of-memory training failure are facts, not an explanation. A machine that crashes and then freezes could be heat, memory pressure, or something else entirely, and I have no measurement from that evening that separates one from the others. "Fixed" and "explained" are two different words. The fleet is fixed. The crash is not explained, and it stays open until something proves it one way or the other.

The verification habit

A server that answers is not a fleet that is back. The morning checklist worked because it checked each piece separately instead of taking one green light as proof of all. The restore on the eleventh worked because it was verified from outside the machine. A machine that comes back is not a machine understood. Booting is not an explanation, and a clean checklist is no diagnosis. The checks tell you the fleet works today — not why it stopped yesterday.
